What water damage is NOT covered by homeowners insurance?

Quick Answer

Homeowners insurance typically excludes flood damage from outside, gradual leaks, sewer backup (without an endorsement), and neglected maintenance issues.

Key Takeaways

  • Standard homeowners insurance covers sudden and accidental water damage — not floodwater from outside, which requires a separate flood insurance policy through NFIP or a private insurer.
  • Gradual damage from a slow leak that went undetected for weeks or months is routinely denied as a maintenance issue, even if you genuinely didn't know about it.
  • Sewer and drain backup is excluded from most standard policies; a separate sewer backup endorsement typically costs $50–$150/year in Colorado and is worth adding before you need it.
  • Colorado Springs spring snowmelt flooding — common in lower-elevation neighborhoods near Fountain Creek and Monument Creek — is classified as surface flooding and requires flood insurance, not standard homeowners coverage.

Why Does It Matter Which Type of Water Damage You Have?

When water is spreading across your floor, the source feels like a detail. It isn't. Whether your claim gets paid — and how much — depends almost entirely on where the water came from and how quickly the damage occurred. Insurers underwrite homeowners policies around sudden, accidental losses that are unpredictable and limited in scope. The exclusions in your policy are not arbitrary; they reflect risk categories that insurers have separated into different products, different premium structures, and sometimes different regulatory frameworks entirely.

The Colorado Division of Insurance (doi.colorado.gov) handles consumer complaints and policy disputes in Colorado. Their published guidance consistently identifies the same four categories of denied water damage claims that adjusters see repeatedly in El Paso County: flooding, gradual damage, sewer backup without an endorsement, and neglected maintenance. Understanding which category your damage falls into — before you file — affects how you document the loss and whether you call your homeowners carrier or your flood insurer first.

The Four Water Damage Exclusions That Cause the Most Denied Claims

  • Surface flooding from outside — rain, snowmelt, rising groundwater
  • Gradual or hidden leaks — slow drips over days, weeks, or months
  • Sewer and drain backup — without a separate endorsement added to your policy
  • Neglected maintenance — damage from a known problem that wasn't repaired

Flood Damage: What "Flood" Means in an Insurance Policy

In everyday language, a flooded basement is a flooded basement. In insurance language, "flood" has a specific legal definition that determines which policy applies — or whether either applies. Standard homeowners policies exclude damage caused by surface water, storm surge, overflow of a body of water, and runoff from saturated ground. Under the National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP) definition, these events require a separate flood insurance policy — either through NFIP or a private flood insurer.

Two seasonal patterns make this exclusion especially consequential in Colorado Springs. First, spring snowmelt: when El Paso County's snowpack melts rapidly — a recurring pattern in March and April at approximately 6,035 feet elevation — it saturates ground that is often still frozen a few inches below the surface. That water moves along grade into basements and crawl spaces, particularly in lower-lying neighborhoods near Fountain Creek and Monument Creek. That is surface flooding. It is excluded from your homeowners policy. Second, summer thunderstorm flooding: Colorado Springs sees significant convective storm activity from June through August, and rapid accumulation on hard or frozen ground produces the same surface flooding dynamic. Neither event is covered under a standard homeowners policy.

⚠️ Watch out: Homeowners in Colorado Springs frequently discover a flooded basement during spring snowmelt and assume it's covered the same way a burst pipe would be. It is not. Groundwater intrusion is a flood exclusion, not an accidental discharge. If your property is in a flood-prone area — including parts of Fountain, Security-Widefield, or neighborhoods adjacent to drainage channels — price a flood insurance policy before next March.

Gradual Damage: The "Maintenance Issue" Denial

This is the exclusion that surprises homeowners most. A slow leak under a kitchen sink or inside a wall cavity can go undetected for weeks — you didn't ignore it, you didn't know it existed. Most homeowners policies still require that water damage be sudden and accidental to be covered. If an adjuster determines the damage accumulated over an extended period, they will typically classify it as a maintenance issue and deny the claim regardless of your awareness.

The IICRC S500 Standard for Professional Water Damage Restoration — the technical document restoration contractors and adjusters use to classify and scope losses — distinguishes between Class 1 losses (limited absorption, affecting only part of a room) and Class 3 losses (maximum absorption throughout walls, ceilings, and floors). Adjusters reviewing a claim examine absorption depth, staining patterns, and mold growth to estimate how long water was present. Per the IICRC S520 Standard for Professional Mold Remediation, mold can begin colonizing wet organic materials — drywall, framing, subfloor — within 24–48 hours at typical room temperatures. Visible mold colonization throughout a wall cavity is evidence an adjuster will use to argue the loss was not sudden.

This is why calling a professional restoration company at the first sign of water damage matters. A contractor providing daily moisture logs — calibrated readings at multiple structural points — creates a contemporaneous record that supports a sudden-loss determination. That documentation is substantially harder to dispute than a verbal account alone.

💡 Tip: If you discover what looks like a slow leak, take date-stamped photos immediately and call a restoration contractor before you call your insurer. Your restoration company's moisture documentation can be your best evidence that you acted promptly upon discovery — which can be the difference between a covered claim and a maintenance-exclusion denial.

Sewer and Drain Backup: The Endorsement Most Colorado Springs Homeowners Don't Have

A sewer backup is among the most damaging and contaminated water events a property can experience. Under the IICRC S500 standard, sewage is classified as Category 3 water: grossly contaminated, containing bacteria, pathogens, and potentially chemical waste. Category 3 material in contact with porous building materials — drywall, insulation, carpet, subfloor — cannot be dried in place. Affected materials must be removed, which significantly increases remediation cost relative to a clean-water pipe failure.

Standard homeowners policies do not cover sewer and drain backup. Coverage requires a separate endorsement, typically called a "water backup" or "sewer backup" rider. According to the Insurance Information Institute (iii.org), this endorsement is one of the most commonly overlooked homeowners policy additions and typically costs $50–$150 per year for $5,000–$10,000 in coverage. A Category 3 sewage cleanup in the Colorado Springs market typically runs $7,000–$15,000, meaning standard endorsement limits may not fully cover the loss — a gap worth evaluating at purchase or renewal.

Colorado Springs has specific infrastructure risk factors for sewer backup. Historic west-side neighborhoods, including Old Colorado City, have older clay tile sewer lines more susceptible to root intrusion and collapse. Heavy spring snowmelt increases municipal sewer system load, creating backpressure into private laterals — the line connecting your home to the city main is your responsibility, not the city's. Properties without backflow prevention valves on their main drain are particularly vulnerable during peak snowmelt months, typically March through May.

Neglected Maintenance: When a Known Problem Becomes an Excluded Loss

Insurance covers the unpredictable, not the inevitable. If a pipe fitting showed signs of corrosion, a water heater past its service life was leaking at the base, or a roof had known unrepaired hail damage — and water subsequently entered through that compromised area — an adjuster has grounds to classify the resulting damage as deferred maintenance. This differs from the gradual-leak exclusion, though adjusters sometimes apply the categories interchangeably.

The practical distinction: gradual damage means the damage itself accumulated slowly. Neglected maintenance means a known defect existed, was not corrected, and its eventual failure caused damage. Either outcome typically produces a denied claim or a substantially reduced settlement. The Colorado Division of Insurance provides a consumer appeals process for wrongly denied claims; their consumer services division is accessible through doi.colorado.gov.

One Colorado Springs-specific maintenance exposure worth noting: at 6,035 feet, freeze-thaw cycles are more aggressive than at lower elevations. Pipes in exterior walls, unheated garages, and crawl spaces face freeze risk from October through April. If a homeowner experienced a prior pipe freeze, did not add insulation or heat tape, and then had a burst pipe in a subsequent season, an adjuster may argue that the prior event created a maintenance obligation. Insurers do not always prevail on this argument, but it is raised often enough to warrant attention.

What IS Covered: Sudden and Accidental Water Damage

Standard homeowners insurance typically covers sudden and accidental water damage from internal sources. A pipe that bursts without warning — including from a freeze event, which is common during Colorado Springs winters — is generally a covered loss under an HO-3 policy. A washing machine supply hose that fails suddenly, an ice maker line that ruptures, a water heater tank that cracks and releases, or a dishwasher that overflows due to mechanical failure are all typically covered events.

The operative criteria: the water originated inside the home's plumbing or appliance system, the failure occurred quickly, and you had no prior reason to anticipate it. Documenting that sequence — with date-stamped photos, a restoration contractor's moisture logs, and a plumber's assessment of the failure point — is how a covered claim remains covered through the adjustment process.

Quick Reference: Covered vs. Not Covered (Standard HO-3 Policy)

  • ✅ Burst pipe (sudden): Typically covered — including freeze-related failures
  • ✅ Appliance failure (sudden): Washing machine, dishwasher, ice maker, water heater rupture
  • ✅ Accidental overflow: Bathtub or sink overflow if sudden and unattended
  • ❌ Surface flooding / snowmelt: Excluded — requires NFIP or private flood insurance
  • ❌ Gradual leaks: Excluded as a maintenance issue regardless of homeowner awareness
  • ❌ Sewer backup: Excluded without a sewer backup endorsement
  • ❌ Neglected maintenance: Excluded if a known defect led to the failure
  • ⚠️ Groundwater intrusion: Typically excluded — classified as flooding

How to Protect Your Claim When You're Not Sure Which Category Applies

Water damage situations are rarely clean-cut. A pipe that froze and burst may have allowed water to migrate under a floor slab and pool at a foundation wall — which an adjuster may read as groundwater intrusion. A sewage backup may have occurred suddenly and without warning, but without a backup endorsement, the sudden nature of the event doesn't change the exclusion. Your first actions after discovering water damage should establish documentation and source clarity before any coverage assumptions are made.

  1. Call a professional restoration contractor immediately. Their moisture documentation — including the date, extent of saturation, and water category classification — begins the contemporaneous record an adjuster will rely on. Every additional hour of saturation changes the evidence base.
  2. Photograph everything before any water is removed. Date-stamped photos showing standing water, the visible source, and affected materials are your primary evidence of conditions at discovery.
  3. Identify the water source before filing. Pipe, appliance, drain, or exterior water — the source determines which policy applies. Filing under the wrong policy wastes time and can complicate a later correction.
  4. Pull your policy and read the exclusions before your first call to your insurer. Look specifically for: flood exclusion language, gradual damage exclusion, and water backup or sewer provisions.
  5. If denied, request the specific policy language cited. The Colorado Division of Insurance requires insurers to cite the exact policy provision supporting a denial. That language is your starting point for a dispute or appraisal.
Bottom Line

Standard homeowners insurance in Colorado Springs does not cover flood damage from outside sources — including spring snowmelt, one of the most common water intrusion events in El Paso County — gradual leaks, sewer backup without an endorsement, or damage from deferred maintenance. What it typically covers is sudden and accidental internal water events: burst pipes, appliance failures, and accidental overflows. The documentation your restoration contractor generates in the first 24 hours often determines whether your claim stays in the covered column. Frequently Asked Questions

Does homeowners insurance cover a burst pipe from freezing in Colorado Springs?

Yes — a frozen pipe that bursts suddenly is generally a covered loss under a standard HO-3 policy, because it meets the "sudden and accidental" requirement. However, if an adjuster determines the pipe was inadequately insulated and you had prior freeze events (which are common in Colorado Springs from October through April at 6,000 ft elevation), they may argue a maintenance exclusion. Document the condition of the pipe with photos and a plumber's assessment immediately after the failure.

Is snowmelt flooding in my basement covered by homeowners insurance?

No. When spring snowmelt saturates the ground and water enters your basement — a common event in Colorado Springs neighborhoods near Fountain Creek and Monument Creek in March and April — that is classified as surface flooding or groundwater intrusion. It is excluded from standard homeowners policies and requires a separate flood insurance policy through the NFIP or a private flood insurer. This is one of the most frequently misunderstood coverage gaps in El Paso County.

What is a sewer backup endorsement and do I need one in Colorado Springs?

A sewer backup (or "water backup") endorsement is an optional addition to a homeowners policy that covers damage from sewer or drain backflow — which is excluded from standard coverage. It typically costs $50–$150 per year. Given that Category 3 sewage cleanup in Colorado Springs commonly runs $7,000–$15,000, and that older neighborhoods like Old Colorado City have aging clay tile sewer infrastructure prone to root intrusion and increased backpressure during snowmelt season, this endorsement is worth adding before you need it.

What counts as "gradual damage" that insurers will deny?

Gradual damage is water damage that accumulated over days, weeks, or months rather than occurring suddenly. A slow drip behind a wall, a weeping pipe joint under a sink, or persistent condensation that rotted a subfloor are examples. Adjusters use moisture meter readings, staining patterns, and mold growth indicators — mold can begin developing within 24–48 hours per the IICRC S520 standard — to estimate how long a loss was active. The longer the estimated duration, the stronger the insurer's argument for a maintenance exclusion.

Does homeowners insurance cover water damage from a washing machine overflow?

Generally yes, if the overflow was sudden and accidental — a supply hose failure or a mechanical malfunction that caused an unattended overflow is typically a covered loss. If the machine overflowed repeatedly and the resulting floor damage was allowed to accumulate, or if there was visible water damage around the machine that was not addressed, an adjuster may treat the loss as gradual or maintenance-related. Document the appliance failure cause immediately.

What if my insurer denies my water damage claim — what can I do in Colorado?

Request the specific policy language the insurer cited as the basis for denial — Colorado law requires insurers to provide this. Review the denial against your actual policy, because adjusters sometimes misapply exclusions. If you believe the denial is incorrect, file a complaint with the Colorado Division of Insurance at doi.colorado.gov. You may also have the right to invoke the appraisal process specified in your policy to dispute the scope or value of a claim, even if coverage is not in dispute.

Is flood insurance required in Colorado Springs?

It is not universally required, but it is required by mortgage lenders for properties located in FEMA-designated Special Flood Hazard Areas (SFHAs). Some areas in El Paso County along Fountain Creek and its tributaries carry FEMA flood zone designations. Even outside designated flood zones, spring snowmelt flooding is a real risk in Colorado Springs, and flood insurance can be purchased voluntarily through the NFIP or a private insurer. Check FEMA's Flood Map Service Center for your specific address.

How does an adjuster determine if water damage was sudden vs. gradual?

Adjusters look at several indicators: the depth of water absorption into building materials (measured with a moisture meter or by physical inspection), staining and efflorescence on concrete, the presence and maturity of mold growth (per IICRC S520, mold colonies visible to the naked eye typically require more than 48 hours to develop), and whether the damage pattern is consistent with a single event or ongoing exposure. This is why professional moisture documentation from a restoration contractor — using calibrated meters with date-stamped logs — is valuable evidence in a sudden-loss determination.
