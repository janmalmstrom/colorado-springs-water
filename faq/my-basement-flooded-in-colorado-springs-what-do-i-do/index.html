My basement flooded in Colorado Springs — what do I do?

Quick Answer

If your basement has flooded in Colorado Springs, stop the water source if it's safe to do so, avoid entering the basement if the water source is unknown or electrical panels are submerged, and call a professional water damage restoration company immediately. Water extraction and structural drying must begin within 24 hours — after 48 hours, mold can begin growing on wet organic materials according to the IICRC S520 standard, and structural damage accelerates rapidly from there.

Key Takeaways

  • Do not enter a flooded basement if electrical panels, outlets, or appliances may be submerged — electrocution is a real risk; shut the breaker first or call your utility company.
  • Identify the water source before anything else: burst pipe, appliance failure, and sewer backup are Category 1, 2, or 3 water — and the cleanup protocol is completely different for each.
  • Mold can begin growing in 24–48 hours on wet drywall, wood framing, and insulation per the IICRC S520 standard — time matters more than most homeowners realize.
  • Colorado Springs basement flooding has three common causes: spring snowmelt infiltration, afternoon thunderstorm surges, and winter freeze-thaw pipe failures — each has different insurance implications.
  • Homeowners insurance typically covers sudden, accidental water damage from inside the home; exterior floodwater requires a separate flood insurance policy through the NFIP or a private carrier.

Step One: Make the Basement Safe Before You Go Down There

The instinct when you discover a flooded basement is to rush downstairs and start moving things. Resist that instinct until you've checked two things: the water source and the electrical panel.

If your electrical panel is in the basement and there is standing water, do not enter. Call your utility provider — Colorado Springs Utilities — and ask them to disconnect power at the meter. Water and live electrical circuits in a confined space is a life-safety emergency that takes priority over everything else.

Once you've confirmed power is off or your panel is above the waterline, look at what's actually wet. Standing water that is dark, has an odor, or came in from a floor drain backing up is not safe to wade through without waterproof boots and gloves at a minimum. Per the IICRC S500 standard, water from unknown sources should be treated as contaminated (Category 2 or Category 3) until proven otherwise.

Identify the Water Source — It Changes Everything

Not all basement flooding is the same. The IICRC S500 standard classifies water damage into three categories based on contamination level, and the category determines what cleanup protocol is required:

  • Category 1 (clean water): Supply line failure, water heater rupture, or pipe burst from a clean source. Lowest contamination risk. This is the most common cause of Colorado Springs basement flooding during the October–April freeze season, when pipes in exterior walls, crawl spaces, and garages are vulnerable to freeze-thaw cycles at our 6,000-foot elevation.
  • Category 2 (gray water): Water from a washing machine overflow, dishwasher discharge, or sump pump failure pulling from a pit with some biological contamination. Requires professional extraction and antimicrobial treatment — not just drying.
  • Category 3 (black water/sewage): Sewer backup, toilet bowl overflow, or floodwater that has entered from outside the structure. Per the IICRC S500 standard, Category 3 materials cannot simply be dried — contaminated porous materials (drywall, carpet, insulation, wood) must be removed and discarded. This is the highest-cost and highest-health-risk scenario.

In Colorado Springs, spring snowmelt events — particularly in lower-elevation neighborhoods near Fountain Creek and its tributaries — can push groundwater through foundation cracks and floor drains. That water, once it's traveled through soil, is treated as Category 3 under IICRC protocols. It is not clean water, regardless of how it looks.

What to Do in the First Hour

Once you've confirmed it's safe to be in the space, here is the priority order:

  • Stop the source if you can. Shut off the main water supply if it's a pipe or appliance failure. If it's groundwater infiltration from a storm, you cannot stop the source — focus on calling for help.
  • Call a professional restoration company. Commercial truck-mounted extraction equipment removes water at a rate that consumer wet-vacs cannot approach. More importantly, a professional will use moisture meters to identify water that has wicked into drywall, subfloor, and framing — water you cannot see or feel.
  • Document everything before anyone touches it. Photograph and video the standing water level, affected contents, and visible structural damage. This documentation is the foundation of your insurance claim. The adjuster was not there — your photos are the record.
  • Call your insurance company. Report the loss and ask about your coverage for water damage, including whether you have a sewer backup endorsement if the source is a drain backup. Do this before any major demolition work begins.
  • Move undamaged contents out of the space if you can do so safely. Dry, moveable items that aren't yet wet are worth protecting. Do not attempt to move furniture through standing Category 2 or 3 water without protection.

Do not run box fans or your home HVAC system in an attempt to dry things out. Without moisture meters calibrated to building materials, you have no way to know whether the air movement is helping or simply pushing moisture further into the structure. Industrial dehumidifiers and air movers used by restoration professionals are calibrated to the specific moisture readings in your materials — consumer equipment is not.

The Mold Clock Is Already Running

Per the IICRC S520 standard on mold remediation, mold can begin growing on wet organic materials — drywall paper facing, wood framing, carpet backing, and insulation — within 24 to 48 hours at room temperature. In a finished basement with drywall, flooring, and insulation, this timeline is not theoretical. It is the standard used by insurance adjusters and industrial hygienists when evaluating whether damage is storm-related or the result of delayed response.

This matters for two reasons. First, your health — mold in a basement HVAC return can circulate spores through your entire home. Second, your claim — insurers can and do dispute mold remediation costs when they argue the mold resulted from delayed response rather than the original water event. Getting professional extraction started within 24 hours protects both.

Structural drying under IICRC S500 standards typically takes 3 to 5 days for a Category 1 loss with proper equipment in place. Category 2 or 3 losses, and losses with significant structural saturation, take longer. A restoration professional will perform moisture readings daily and document the drying progress — that log is part of your insurance file.

Understanding Your Insurance Coverage for Basement Flooding

Colorado Springs homeowners frequently discover a gap in coverage at the worst possible moment. Here is what most standard homeowners policies actually cover, and what they do not:

  • Covered (typically): Sudden and accidental water damage from inside the home — burst pipes, water heater failure, washing machine supply line rupture. This is the most common type of coverage for freeze-related burst pipes in winter.
  • Not covered under standard homeowners policy: Surface water flooding that enters from outside the structure (storm runoff, creek overflow, groundwater). For this, you need a separate flood insurance policy through FEMA's National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP) or a private flood carrier. The NFIP has a standard 30-day waiting period before coverage begins — it cannot be purchased after a storm event is already occurring. Visit FEMA's flood insurance page for current NFIP rates and coverage details.
  • Sewer backup: This is a separate endorsement on most Colorado homeowners policies. A standard policy does not cover sewage that backs up through a floor drain or toilet. If you've had drain backups before, check your policy now — before you need it. The Colorado Division of Insurance maintains consumer resources if you need help understanding your policy or filing a complaint about a claim denial.
  • Gradual damage: Slow leaks that built up over weeks or months are typically excluded as a maintenance issue. Insurance covers events, not deferred maintenance.

If you're unsure what your policy covers, call your agent and ask specifically about: sudden water damage, sewer backup endorsement, and flood insurance. Those are three separate conversations. We can provide detailed moisture documentation and a scope of loss to your adjuster — but coverage determinations are made by your insurer, not by us.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it safe to go into a flooded basement?
Not until you've confirmed there's no electrical hazard. If your electrical panel is in the basement or there are submerged outlets or appliances, shut the main breaker or call Colorado Springs Utilities to disconnect power at the meter before entering. Once electrical safety is confirmed, treat unknown water sources as contaminated (IICRC S500 Category 2 or 3) until you know the source — wear waterproof boots and gloves at minimum.
How long does basement flood cleanup take in Colorado Springs?
Water extraction typically takes several hours depending on volume. Structural drying — the process of removing moisture from drywall, framing, subfloor, and insulation — typically takes 3 to 5 days for a Category 1 (clean water) loss with commercial drying equipment in place, per IICRC S500 standards. Category 2 or 3 losses, or losses with significant structural saturation, take longer. Your restoration technician will perform daily moisture readings and can give you a timeline based on actual readings from your structure.
Does homeowners insurance cover basement flooding in Colorado Springs?
It depends entirely on the source of the water. Homeowners insurance typically covers sudden and accidental water damage from inside the home — burst pipes, appliance failures, water heater rupture. It does not cover floodwater that enters from outside the structure; that requires a separate flood insurance policy through FEMA's NFIP or a private carrier. Sewer backup coverage is also a separate endorsement on most Colorado policies. Call your agent and ask specifically about all three.
What is the difference between flood insurance and homeowners insurance for my basement?
Homeowners insurance covers internal water events (pipe bursts, appliance failures). Flood insurance, available through FEMA's National Flood Insurance Program or private carriers, covers surface water that enters your home from outside — storm runoff, overflowing waterways, or groundwater from significant rain events. Colorado Springs sits in a region where spring snowmelt and afternoon thunderstorms can push surface water into basements; that water is typically not covered by a standard homeowners policy. NFIP policies have a 30-day waiting period, so they cannot be purchased reactively.
How quickly can mold grow after a basement flood?
Per the IICRC S520 standard on mold remediation, mold can begin growing on wet organic materials — drywall, wood framing, carpet, and insulation — within 24 to 48 hours at room temperature. This is the industry-standard timeframe used by adjusters and industrial hygienists. It is why professional water extraction needs to begin within the first 24 hours when possible. Delayed response that results in mold growth can complicate insurance claims and significantly increase total remediation costs.
Can I clean up basement flood water myself?
For Category 1 (clean water) losses covering a very small area, homeowners can attempt extraction with a wet-vac — but consumer equipment cannot match the extraction rate of commercial truck-mounted units, and has no way to address moisture absorbed into drywall, subfloor, and framing. For Category 2 (gray water) or Category 3 (black water/sewage) losses, professional remediation is strongly recommended. Contaminated water requires antimicrobial treatment and, in Category 3 situations, the removal of all porous materials that absorbed the contamination. Attempting DIY cleanup on sewage backup creates serious health risks and can void insurance coverage.
Why is Colorado Springs at higher risk for basement flooding from frozen pipes?
Colorado Springs sits at approximately 6,000 feet elevation, and the freeze-thaw cycle runs from October through April. Pipes in exterior walls, garages, crawl spaces, and areas with insufficient insulation are particularly vulnerable. When temperatures drop rapidly overnight and rebound during the day — a common Front Range weather pattern — pipes can freeze and burst during the night, and the damage may not be discovered until morning. The volume of water from an undetected burst supply line can be significant within a few hours.
What does IICRC certification mean for a water damage restoration company?
IICRC stands for the Institute of Inspection, Cleaning and Restoration Certification. It is the primary voluntary industry certification body that sets technical standards for water damage restoration (S500), mold remediation (S520), and related services. IICRC-certified technicians have completed formal training and testing in restoration science, moisture measurement, and drying protocols. It is not a government license, but it is the standard that insurance adjusters and property managers use to evaluate whether a restoration contractor is following accepted industry practices. Hiring an IICRC-certified company helps ensure your documentation will hold up with your insurer.
