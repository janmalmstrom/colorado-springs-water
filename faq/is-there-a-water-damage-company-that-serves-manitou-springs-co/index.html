Why Manitou Springs homes have higher water damage risk than newer Colorado Springs construction

Manitou Springs was developed primarily between 1880 and 1950 as a resort and residential community around the natural mineral springs. Most of the housing stock is 70–140 years old. Galvanized steel pipe — common in homes built before the 1960s — corrodes from the inside out over decades. As the pipe walls thin, pinhole leaks develop and progress to catastrophic failures. Homeowners often discover the problem only when returning home to standing water.

At 6,412 ft elevation, Manitou Springs experiences harder freeze cycles than Colorado Springs proper. Pipes routed through exterior walls, unheated crawl spaces, or the stone foundations typical of Victorian-era construction are exposed to sustained below-freezing temperatures that supply lines in a modern, well-insulated home typically would not face. Under the IICRC S500 Standard for Professional Water Damage Restoration, the water source determines the contamination category — a burst supply line is Category 1 (clean water), while a backed-up drain or toilet overflow is Category 2 or Category 3, each requiring progressively more aggressive extraction and antimicrobial treatment.

⚠️ Watch out: Older Manitou Springs homes often have partial plumbing updates — one section replaced with copper, another still running galvanized. A contractor unfamiliar with mixed-era systems may miss a compromised section that later fails. Ensure whoever you call performs a full pipe assessment, not just repair of the visible break.

What happens when Fountain Creek floods — and why it matters for insurance

Fountain Creek is not a theoretical risk. The creek runs along the base of the canyon directly through Manitou Springs and has flooded multiple times in recorded history, most significantly during the 2013 Front Range flood event that caused widespread damage across El Paso County. When Fountain Creek overflows its banks or storm runoff from Ruxton Creek and Williams Creek overtops the canyon drainage, the water entering a home is exterior floodwater.

Under IICRC S500, exterior floodwater is classified as Category 3 (black water) — the highest contamination category. It carries sediment, sewage, agricultural runoff, and pathogens. Category 3 material cannot be dried in place; porous materials that have absorbed Category 3 water must be removed entirely. Drywall, insulation, carpet, and subflooring in a flooded Manitou Springs basement are treated as contaminated waste.

Standard homeowners insurance does not cover exterior floodwater. Fountain Creek flooding requires a separate flood insurance policy — either through FEMA's National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP) or a private flood insurer. If you are a Manitou Springs property owner without flood insurance, water entering from the creek during a storm event is almost certainly not a covered loss under your homeowners policy. The contractors we connect you with will document the water source and damage category accurately for your adjuster — but coverage is determined by your policy.

💡 Tip: Manitou Springs properties within the Fountain Creek floodplain may be in FEMA-designated flood zones. Check your property's flood zone status at the FEMA Flood Map Service Center (msc.fema.gov). Properties in Zone AE or Zone A typically require flood insurance if they carry a federally backed mortgage.

What the structural drying process looks like inside a Manitou Springs home

The restoration process for a Manitou Springs property follows the IICRC S500 framework, though several local factors affect execution.

Extraction. Commercial truck-mounted extraction equipment removes standing water from flooring and accessible cavities. In stone-foundation Victorian homes common on the west end of Manitou Avenue, crawl space water accumulates in areas requiring specialized equipment — this is not a job for a consumer shop vac.

Moisture mapping. Restoration technicians use thermal imaging cameras and calibrated moisture meters to identify water migration behind plaster walls, under hardwood floors, and inside stone or brick foundation walls. Water travels differently in pre-1950 construction than in modern stud-framed homes — lath-and-plaster walls and original hardwood floors retain moisture differently than drywall and laminate.

Structural drying. Industrial dehumidifiers and air movers are positioned and calibrated to achieve drying goals set by IICRC S500 psychrometric calculations. At 6,412 ft elevation, the ambient air is drier than at sea level — which can accelerate surface drying while interior structural moisture remains elevated. Technicians take daily moisture readings and log them to confirm structural drying and provide documentation for insurance claims.

Timeline. For a straightforward Category 1 loss in typical conditions, structural drying takes 3–5 days. Category 2 or Category 3 losses, or situations where water has been present for more than 24–48 hours before discovery, require longer periods. Per IICRC S520 Standard for Professional Mold Remediation, mold can begin developing on wet organic materials within 24–48 hours at room temperature — which is why rapid response is critical.
